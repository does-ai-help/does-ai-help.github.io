# Assessment Validity, AI Detection and Educational Restraint

Appraised 12 November 2026 for the November draft. This targeted follow-up adds nine bibliographic records, not nine independent empirical studies. It does not establish a worldwide trend in university policy.

## Primary institutional evidence

The reader-suggested [Gizmodo article](https://gizmodo.com/universities-are-giving-up-on-trying-to-detect-ai-cheating-2000838311), by Ece Yildirim, leads to the [OfS/Advance HE report](https://www.officeforstudents.org.uk/publications/making-ai-work-for-students-reflections-on-institutional-practice/), published 11 November. Its engagement included 244 staff and 91 students/student representatives from at least 127 institutions, with 64 online responses and 22 interviews. Participation was self-selecting; these are not representative prevalence estimates or causal learning results. The regulatory context is England, not the entire UK.

The report accommodates secure and AI-permitted assessment, accessibility needs and principled non-use. It does not prescribe abandoning all detection or take-home work. [Commentary by the report's institutional leaders](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/2026/11/11/making-ai-work-for-students/) concerns the same exercise, not replication.

**Our interpretation:** the object of assessment is competence, not merely the provenance of a submitted artifact. A detector can therefore perform better than chance without solving the educational problem. Conversely, an AI-free examination is not automatically a valid or accessible assessment of every professional skill.

## Detector evidence: avoid either categorical claim

[Van Vlasselaer, Van Droogenbroeck and Spruyt](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-026-00226-w) compare four detectors on 160 controlled documents, including human, generated and mixed categories. Pangram performs comparatively well within that design. This is useful counterevidence to the proposition that every detector is invariably useless. It is not a November product ranking: tool versions, thresholds, scoring rules and the small controlled corpus constrain transfer.

The study also applies detection to 1,163 unlabeled master's theses. Its 45.5% flag rate is **not a ground-truth estimate of cheating or AI authorship**. Individual misconduct requires evidence about authorship, permitted use and the actual assessment rules.

The [6 October correction](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40979-026-00236-8) identifies the fully generated category as ChatGPT Deep Research using an o3-based model, not GPT-4o. The authors state that analyses and conclusions are unchanged. The original and correction share a study-family identifier; the correction is not a new experiment.

[Perkins and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-024-00487-w) provide an older robustness experiment. Generation dates to 2023 and the short constrained samples do not establish current detector performance. The abstract and body give different detector counts; we do not turn that inconsistency into a universal accuracy figure. The [versioned dataset description](https://doi.org/10.17632/xv6fk2mmh9.3) was checked, but its files were not reanalyzed. We do not reproduce evasion instructions.

## Product behavior is not student learning

Common Sense Media's [ChatGPT for Teens assessment](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/risk-assessments/chatgpt-teens), updated 7 October, examines selected accounts, safeguards and homework behavior. Its [Google Search assessment](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/risk-assessments/google-search), updated 14 July, includes 180 homework assignments and reports complete responses from AI Mode throughout that battery. Completion is not correctness, improved understanding or observed cheating. Neither assessment is a randomized educational trial. Evaluator risk categories are judgments under a declared framework, not population harm rates. Funding and asserted independence are disclosed; product changes require rechecking.

## Policies document choices, not effects

The [New York City announcement](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat), dated 2 September, concerns a one-year student-facing generative-AI moratorium from 2K through grade 8, alongside limited high-school provision. [Norway's 19 June announcement](https://www.regjeringen.no/no/aktuelt/kunstig-intelligens-skal-i-all-hovedsak-ikke-brukes-i-barneskolen/id3166807/) sets age-tiered recommendations and preserves accommodations. It should not be described as a universal statutory ban. The Norwegian original was read; the English characterization here is a paraphrase, not an official translation.

These are important institutional objections and constraints. They are not proof that the policies work, nor grounds for transferring school-age restrictions to university teaching or adult work.

The linked Atlantic detector article was only partly accessible; its substantive claims are not imported. Several linked secondary pages failed to load, so primary organization and government records were located separately. Image-recognition comparisons and mental-health/legal allegations were not imported into this assessment appraisal. The evidence database retains dates, reading scopes, DOI identifiers, source-family relationships and the report checksum. Original copyrighted documents are not redistributed.
